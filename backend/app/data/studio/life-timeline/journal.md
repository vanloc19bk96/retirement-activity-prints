rest of the page left blank for a handwritten answer.

The reader opens the journal at a fresh page, reads one line, and starts
writing. That line has to be easy to begin — and worth a whole page.

EVERY PROMPT MUST:
- Be ONE short question about the reader's OWN life — what they remember,
  noticed, did, or would like.
- Open a page rather than close it: answerable in a line, yet with more to say
  if they want to keep writing.
- Name ONE concrete thing they can picture — a room, a meal, a smell, a sound,
  a walk, an object, something a person always did — not a topic in the
  abstract.
- Ask about one thing only: no "a smell or a sound", no "the kitchen or the
  garden". Choose the one that fits the theme and ask about that.
- Read like something said out loud, kindly, by someone sitting beside them.
- Have no right answer and nothing to look up: no dates, no full names, no
  counting, no "can you remember". Whatever comes to mind is a correct answer.
- Fit any reader: assume nothing about who they had, where they lived, what
  they owned, or what they can still do. Ask openly, so anyone can answer.
- Stand completely on its own — it appears alone, with no other prompt on the
  page and nothing before or after it to lean on.
- Stay under {max_len} characters, so it sets in large type on two lines.

THE SET OF {needed} MUST:
- Give every prompt a different angle. Take angles from this list, and use each
  at most once:
  - a person and one thing they always said, wore or did
  - a room, a corner or a view known by heart
  - a smell, a sound, a taste or something under the hand
  - an object kept, used, mended, borrowed or handed on
  - food: cooked, shared, saved for a Sunday, or looked forward to
  - an ordinary day at its edges — first thing in the morning, last thing at night
  - one particular occasion: an arrival, an outing, a visitor, a surprise
  - a habit, a knack or a small routine, and who it came from
  - weather and seasons, and what they changed about the day
  - words: a saying, a nickname, a tune, a programme that was always on
  - a small pleasure available today, this week
  - something to look forward to, however ordinary
- Vary the opening word — no more than two prompts may begin with the same word.
- Use "favourite", "best" or "most" in at most ONE prompt in the set.
- Vary the shape and the length: not one mould filled {needed} times.

UNMISTAKABLY THIS THEME — the hardest part, and where these prompts usually fail:
- Put the theme inside the question — its people, places, things and doings —
  instead of naming it like a heading.
- Test each prompt on its own, with the theme covered up. An ordinary reader
  must still see which theme it came from. If it would sit just as happily
  under any other theme, it is not finished: rewrite it.

TOO GENERIC — never write anything like these:
- "What is your favourite memory?" (asks for a summary of a whole life)
- "What do you enjoy?" (nothing concrete in it to picture)
- "Tell us about your family." (not a question, and not one thing)
- "What year did you move house?" (a test, with a right answer)

SPECIFIC ENOUGH — this is the level wanted. Match the size and the grip of
these, anchor your own to the theme, and never copy the wording:
- "What did your kitchen smell like on a baking day?"
- "Which chair in the house was always yours?"
- "What is the first sound you heard this morning?"
- "Who taught you to make something you still make the same way?"
